A stage rally is planned on public roads across the Yorkshire Dales on Sunday 26th March 2028, which is also Mothering Sunday. Roads on the route are expected to be closed to normal traffic for the event, affecting residents, farms, businesses and visitors in many communities.

This site does not take a side for or against the rally. It sets out two things that every resident, landowner and regular visitor should know:

  1. How to make sure you are consulted when the organisers apply for permission.
  2. What the law says about pedestrian access to homes and other premises while roads are closed.

Key dates

  • Now: register with Motorsport UK to be consulted (see below). Registration lasts 12 months, so it must be renewed each year.
  • About June 2027: Motorsport UK has said that permit applications usually arrive about nine months before an event.
  • By 26th September 2027 at the latest: the law requires the application for a motor race order to reach the highway authority at least six months before the event (section 12C(4), Road Traffic Act 1988).
  • Sunday 26th March 2028: the proposed date of the rally.

1. Ask to be consulted by Motorsport UK

Before a rally on public roads can go ahead, the organisers need a permit from Motorsport UK, the governing body for the sport. Before Motorsport UK issues that permit, the law requires it to consult certain people, and that includes anyone who has asked in writing to be consulted.

Road Traffic Act 1988, section 12B(2)

Before issuing a permit, the motor sport governing body must consult—

…

(e) each person who has given the motor sport governing body written notice within the previous 12 months that the person wishes to be consulted about applications under this section, and

What this means in plain English

  • If you send Motorsport UK a written notice (an email is fine), it must consult you before it issues a permit.
  • The notice only counts for 12 months. Put a reminder in your diary to send it again each year until the event.
  • This is a right to be consulted. The Act does not expressly oblige Motorsport UK to tell you when an application arrives, and Motorsport UK has said it has no duty to notify residents. The template below therefore asks to be told as well.

A template you can send

Copy the text below into an email, fill in the parts in square brackets, and send it to David.Powell@motorsportuk.org, who handles Closed Road applications for Motorsport UK.

To: David.Powell@motorsportuk.org
Subject: Written notice under section 12B(2)(e) Road Traffic Act 1988: Yorkshire Dales Rally, Sunday 26th March 2028

Dear Mr Powell,

I am writing to give Motorsport UK written notice that I wish to be consulted about applications for organising permits under section 12B of the Road Traffic Act 1988.

Section 12B(2)(e) provides that, before issuing a permit, the motor sport governing body must consult each person who has given it written notice within the previous 12 months that the person wishes to be consulted about applications under that section.

I live at [your full address], which is on, or close to, the likely route of the proposed Yorkshire Dales Rally on Sunday 26th March 2028. Please register me as a person to be consulted about any application for a permit for that event, and for any other event in this area.

I should also be grateful if you would tell me when an application for this event is received, and confirm that you have registered this notice.

I understand I will need to renew this request in one calendar year.

Yours sincerely,

[Your name]

Your address is needed so that Motorsport UK can see you are affected; it is not published anywhere.

Tell your local representatives too

Your parish council and your North Yorkshire councillor (sometimes still called your county councillor) can raise residents' concerns with the highway authority, and parish councils are among the bodies Motorsport UK must consult. A short email letting them know you have registered, and asking them to keep you informed, is worthwhile. See Who to contact for how to find them.

2. How the road closures are authorised

Three separate permissions are involved. It helps to know which body is responsible for which.

1

Permit

Motorsport UK issues a permit to the organisers under section 12B of the Road Traffic Act 1988. This is where your right to be consulted applies.

2

Motor race order

The highway authority (North Yorkshire Council) decides whether to make a motor race order under section 12D. This lifts ordinary road traffic laws, such as speed limits, for the competitors.

3

Road closure order

The highway authority makes a separate order under section 16A of the Road Traffic Regulation Act 1984. This is the order that actually closes the roads.

The motor race order does not itself close any road. The application for it must be accompanied by details of any section 16A orders that will be needed (section 12C(5)(b)). So the rules on section 16A orders, including the rule on pedestrian access explained below, are central to whether, and how, the event can be held.

Before making a motor race order, the council must also consider the likely impact of the event on the local community, and must be satisfied that effective arrangements have been made to involve local residents in its planning (section 12D(1) and (2)).

3. Pedestrian access to premises: section 16A(9)

Section 16A allows a council to close a road for an event, including to people on foot. Section 16A(2) gives the power to restrict or prohibit use of the road:

Road Traffic Regulation Act 1984, section 16A(2) (extract)

…the authority may by order restrict or prohibit temporarily the use of that road, or any part of it, by vehicles or vehicles of any class or by pedestrians, to such extent and subject to such conditions or exceptions as they may consider necessary or expedient.

But that power has a firm limit. Section 16A(9) ends with these words:

Road Traffic Regulation Act 1984, section 16A(9) (closing words)

…but no such order shall be made with respect to any road which would have the effect of preventing at any time access for pedestrians to any premises situated on or adjacent to the road, or to any other premises accessible for pedestrians from, and only from, the road.

The legal meaning

  • It is a restriction on the council, not a permission for the public. The words "no such order shall be made" limit what the highway authority may lawfully do. The council has no discretion to make an order that breaches them.
  • It overrides the power to close the road to pedestrians. However widely section 16A(2) is used, no order may have the effect of preventing pedestrian access to the premises described.
  • "At any time" means at all times. The test is not whether access is available for most of the day, or between stages, but whether the order would prevent it at any moment while it is in force.
  • It looks at the effect of the order, not the intention behind it. If, in practice, a person on foot could not reach the premises, the order has that effect.
  • It covers three kinds of premises: those on the road; those adjacent to it; and any other premises that can be reached on foot only from that road, such as a house at the end of a track that leaves the closed road.

In plain English

The council can close a road for a rally, and can stop people walking along it. What it cannot do is make a closure that stops someone on foot from getting to a home, a farm, a church or any other premises on or next to that road, or reached only from it. That applies for the whole time the closure is in force.

What section 16A(9) is not

It is not a general right to walk along a closed road or to stand on a rally stage. Someone who simply wants to stroll along the route, or to watch, gains nothing from section 16A(9). The protection is for access to premises.

Equally, it is not a matter for negotiation or goodwill. It sets a limit on the power to make the order at all.

Escorts and chaperones

Organisers of road events sometimes offer to escort residents through a closure with a marshal, or to let them through "when it is safe". This can be helpful, but it does not, in our reading of the Act, meet the requirement of section 16A(9):

  • The duty is placed on the order, which must not have the effect of preventing access at any time. An offer of an escort is an arrangement by the organisers, not a term of the order, and it depends on a marshal being available.
  • If a pedestrian must wait until a marshal decides it is safe, then for that period access is prevented. The Act says "at any time".
  • An escort offered at the discretion of the event can be withdrawn, delayed or refused. The statutory protection cannot.

Anyone who is told that their access will depend on an escort may wish to ask the council how the proposed order complies with section 16A(9).

4. Examples

These everyday situations show why the rule matters, especially on Mothering Sunday.

Visiting Mum

A family arrives to take their mother out for Mothering Sunday lunch. They park outside the closure and walk the last few hundred yards to her house. Her home is on the closed road, so the order must not prevent them reaching it on foot.

Going to church

A resident wants to walk to a Mothering Sunday service, or a visitor wants to walk to a church or chapel on, or reached only from, the closed road. The building is "premises", and pedestrian access to it is protected.

A carer's visit

A home carer must reach an elderly client at a set time for medication. The carer can leave the car outside the closure, but the order must not stop them walking to the door.

A farm or smallholding

A farmer needs to reach farm buildings at the end of a track that leaves the closed road, for example to attend to lambing ewes in late March. Premises reached only from the road are covered.

Holiday guests

Guests arrive at a holiday cottage or bed and breakfast on the route. They may not be able to drive to the door during the closure, but they must be able to walk to it.

Coming home

A resident returns from a walk, or is dropped off outside the closure, and walks home. Their own home is plainly premises on the road.
